Water on Earth is always moving. The Sun heats the surface of oceans, rivers and lakes, and this heat turns liquid water into an invisible gas called water vapour. This slow change from liquid to gas is called evaporation. Plants also add water vapour to the air through their leaves in a process called transpiration. One large tree can move hundreds of litres of water into the air on a hot day.

As warm air rises it cools down. Cool air cannot hold as much water vapour as warm air, so some of the vapour changes back into tiny droplets. This change from gas to liquid is called condensation. The droplets are so small that they float, and billions of them together form a cloud. Dust and sea salt give the droplets a surface to form on, which is why clouds are common over cities and oceans.

Inside a cloud, droplets bump into each other and join together. When the drops become heavy enough, gravity pulls them down as rain, snow, sleet or hail. Any water that falls from clouds is called precipitation. Rain that reaches the ground can do three things: it can run over the surface into streams and rivers, it can soak into the soil, or it can be taken up again by plants.

Water that soaks into the ground is called groundwater. It fills the small spaces between grains of soil and rock, and it moves slowly downhill. People dig wells to reach groundwater, and in many villages it is the only source of drinking water all year. Water that runs over the surface, called runoff, carries soil and nutrients into rivers and finally back to the sea.

The same water has been cycling for billions of years. The water in your glass may once have been part of a cloud, a glacier or a dinosaur. Because the total amount of water on Earth stays almost the same, the cycle is a closed loop, but where the water is at any moment can change. A drought happens when a region gets much less rain than usual for a long time, so rivers shrink and groundwater is not refilled. Floods happen when too much rain arrives too quickly for rivers and soil to carry it away.

Human activity changes the cycle. Cities cover soil with concrete, so less water soaks in and more runs off. Cutting down forests removes the trees that would have released water vapour, and a warmer atmosphere can hold more vapour, which can make heavy rainfall heavier.
